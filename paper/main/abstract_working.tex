Recent evidence links generative-AI exposure to weaker employment among workers in their early twenties. I rebuild this comparison in public monthly CPS data and ask which occupational contrasts support it. The fully reconstructed estimate comparing the highest and lowest GPT-4-exposure quintiles is $-0.132$ log points (95-percent occupation wild-score interval $[-0.221,-0.044]$). Once broad occupation families receive their own young-relative monthly paths, the estimate is $-0.022$ ($[-0.161,0.117]$); the paired movement is $0.110$ ($[0.011,0.210]$). Direct within-family support spans only 29 occupations and is too imprecise to resolve the residual gradient. Controlling for preperiod computer use makes the association more negative rather than attenuating it, so computerization is not a simple omitted-variable explanation. Unrestricted quarterly dynamics reject joint preperiod equality, and corrected CPS flows do not distinguish exit, entry allocation, or reported occupational switching. The public-CPS pattern is therefore a descriptive comparison across broad occupational structure, not an identified causal effect of AI or a public replication of employer hiring results.
