\section{Timing, Weights, and Remaining Interpretations}\label{sec:reallocation}

\subsection{Pre-AI pseudo-breaks and post-period dynamics}

The authenticated extract begins in January 2017, so the literal request for monthly pseudo-breaks beginning in 2015 is infeasible. I estimate every feasible break in the fully pre-AI January 2017--December 2019 window and emphasize 12 dates with at least 12 retained months on each side. Under both pre-AI classifications and the frozen classification, the balanced pseudo-break distributions are centered near zero; none approaches $-0.1311$. Because the 12 estimates overlap and were designed after the headline was known, the plus-one empirical tail of $1/13$ is a resolution floor, not a randomization $p$-value. Unbalanced early-window extremes are artifacts of having as little as one pre-break month.

The actual post path remains descriptive rather than a causal event study. A model with separate periods gives $-0.1356$ in 2023, $-0.0783$ in 2024, and $-0.1629$ in 2025--26. The last is distinguishable from 2024 but not from 2023. No detected pretrend and an end-2022 break do not isolate AI from the technology-sector correction, interest rates, return-to-office changes, post-pandemic normalization, or occupational composition.

I do not add a national adoption series merely to replace the post indicator. Interacting one national series with fixed occupation exposure would largely reparameterize an already displayed exposure-by-time path. Industry-level BTOS adoption would require an occupation--industry ecological bridge and would still not generate exogenous occupational adoption. Such data could support a separately designed adoption paper, but forcing it into this measurement-centered revision would change both treatment and estimand without resolving the main confound.

\subsection{Population controls and response composition}

January 2025 introduced unusually large CPS population-control revisions. A literal age-by-occupation counterfactual series ``without'' those controls cannot be constructed: BLS provides aggregate experimental adjustments, not subgroup weights, and official earlier microdata are not revised. I therefore report what the available data identify rather than rescale cells ad hoc.

On the repaired calendar, official weights give $-0.1346$ and respondent-equivalent cells give $-0.1348$. Ending in December 2024 gives $-0.1135$ and $-0.1056$, respectively. In a joint official-weight model, 2023--24 is $-0.1108$ and 2025--26 is $-0.1664$; their paired difference is $-0.0556$ with interval $[-0.1228,0.0116]$. Under respondent equivalents the era difference is more negative and detected. The raw official-weight Q5--Q1 log-ratio contrast becomes slightly less negative from December 2024 to January 2025. These checks do not provide a counterfactual weight vintage, but they are inconsistent with revised population weights mechanically creating the full association.

``Respondent equivalent'' means that cells are reconstructed from observed employed records after route expansion without their CPS final weights. It changes the empirical cell outcome; dividing all weighted stocks by a common constant would not change the maximizer and is not what is done here.

\subsection{Age, entry, and mobility}

The pooled older comparison is not the sole source of the pattern. Ages 22--25 versus 26--35 give $-0.1307$, nearly identical to the frozen pooled result. Comparisons with ages 26--45 and 36--55 are $-0.1475$ and $-0.1464$. The contrast against ages 51--65 is smaller, $-0.0978$, and its paired difference from the pooled comparison is detected. Age 22--25 is an age group, not observed experience; the paper therefore uses ``young'' rather than treating every respondent as a labor-market entrant.

The main outcome cannot determine whether hiring, separation, or occupation switching generated the stock association. CPS longitudinal links do allow distinct flow estimands, but their intervals are wide. Entry destination is an allocation conditional on moving from nonemployment to employment, not an employment-finding probability. The employed-to-employed switch frame says still less about hiring. For that reason, the mobility analysis, family-balanced disagreement statistics, rematching benchmark, and flow regressions appear in the online appendix rather than as a main contribution. No immediate-reversal rate is treated as a validated occupation-coding-error rate.
