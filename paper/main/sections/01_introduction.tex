\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Recent evidence has linked occupational exposure to generative artificial intelligence with weaker employment growth among workers in their early twenties. The pattern matters because it bears on whether new technology is already changing entry-level work. It is also unusually hard to interpret. Occupational exposure is fixed before the labor outcome is observed, is highly correlated with broad occupational structure, and is available in several constructed forms that do not necessarily measure the same object. A negative exposed-versus-unexposed contrast can therefore reflect detailed AI susceptibility, broad occupational composition, or both.

This paper asks a deliberately narrower question: \emph{does a detailed occupational AI score explain the public-CPS early-career employment pattern beyond the evolution of broad occupation families?} I aggregate Basic Monthly Current Population Survey records into occupation-by-age-by-month employment stocks from January 2017 through July 2026. The design compares ages 22--25 with ages 26--65 within occupations and asks whether that relative stock changes after January 2023 across exposure groups. The outcome is a stock, not hiring, and the design is descriptive. It does not observe employer adoption and does not turn the release of ChatGPT into an instrument.

The corrected-calendar baseline reproduces a sizeable pattern. The post-January-2023 young-relative coefficient for the highest versus lowest employment-weighted quintile of Eloundou et al.'s GPT-4 beta exposure is $-0.1346$ log points, with a canonical 9,999-draw 95-percent wild-score interval of $[-0.2223,-0.0468]$. The frozen 108-month chronology gives $-0.1311$ and its canonical interval $[-0.2171,-0.0451]$. These numbers describe the change in a young-to-older employment-stock ratio, not a percentage decline in individual employment probability.

The central result changes the interpretation. Adding two-digit-occupation-family by young by post interactions reduces the corrected coefficient to $-0.0315$ with interval $[-0.1676,0.1046]$. Allowing every broad family its own young-relative monthly path gives $-0.0317$. The paired baseline-to-conditioned movement is about $0.103$ log points and its interval excludes zero. Yet this is not an additive decomposition: the conditioning model asks whether detailed quintile status predicts outcomes among the comparisons left after broad-family evolution is absorbed. Only four of 22 broad families contain both Q1 and Q5, and conditional target information falls to 30 percent of the baseline. The evidence says that the baseline is predominantly a comparison across broad occupation families and that the remaining within-family association is weakly measured. It does not assign a causal share to broad composition.

Nor does a simple food-service story account for the result. Excluding low-exposure food-service occupations attenuates the coefficient by 8.8 percent, to $-0.1196$. Excluding food, personal-care, and protective-service occupations makes it slightly more negative. Jointly removing the five occupations with the largest prior leave-one-out movements yields $-0.1011$, while symmetric trimming and continuous down-weighting return estimates close to baseline. Influence is consequential and signed in both directions; ``food-service recovery explains the pattern'' is not supported.

The second result concerns precision. The realized occupation-clustered standard error is 0.0444, compared with 0.0122 in the planning simulation. The frozen design's normal-theory minimum detectable effect at 80-percent power is 0.1244 log points, approximately the size of the headline coefficient. Paired minimum detectable differences across alternative exposure constructions range from 0.0609 to 0.1689. Every paired Q5--Q1 interval comparing beta with an alternative contains zero. The data therefore do not detect architecture differences at conventional levels; they also do not establish that the architectures are economically equivalent. This distinction replaces earlier language that treated smaller or insignificant alternative point estimates as evidence that the conclusion did not generalize.

The third result connects the public-data exercise directly to \citet{brynjolfsson2026canaries}. Implementing their published GPT-4 beta classification---the top two exposure quintiles against the bottom three---inside the same CPS model yields $-0.0728$ (interval $[-0.1240,-0.0216]$). This is a reproducible component of their design, not a replication of proprietary ADP outcomes, job-title mapping, firm controls, hiring, or separation. Reapplying the grouping across exposure architectures produces coefficients between approximately $-0.088$ and $-0.008$ on common support; only beta's difference from the OECD capability measure is detected under that grouping. The contrast itself is part of the empirical object.

Measurement still matters, but in more specific ways than the earlier manuscript claimed. A literal SOC-2010 exact-code merge into post-2018 data covers only 3.33 percent of computer-and-mathematical employment; the documented taxonomy repair covers 97.7 percent. Most of the associated coefficient change comes from readmitting occupations rather than changing exposure values on fixed support. The original six implementations also span little independent variation: two weighted principal components explain 96.11 percent of their common-support variance. These facts motivate a taxonomy that separates implementation errors, same-construct measurement changes, construct changes, support changes, estimand changes, representation changes, and pure reparameterizations.

The paper contributes a public-data diagnosis of a prominent descriptive pattern and a disciplined way to audit constructed treatments. Its substantive conclusion is negative but informative: in the CPS, detailed AI quintiles do not carry a precisely estimated association after broad occupational evolution is absorbed. Its measurement conclusion is equally bounded: alternative constructed scores can differ in coverage and classification while available outcomes remain too noisy to distinguish many downstream coefficients. The paper does not estimate a causal AI effect, infer economic equivalence from a confidence interval containing zero, or claim that broad-family conditioning identifies the non-AI counterfactual.

The remainder of the paper defines the construction layers, documents the corrected data and inferential target, presents the broad-family result, and then evaluates architecture precision, reference groups, influence, population controls, and timing. Mobility and algebraic reparameterizations are demoted to the online appendix because they do not resolve the employment-stock question.
