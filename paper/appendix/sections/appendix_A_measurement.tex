\section{Exposure architectures and measurement lineage}\label{app:measurement}

The six architectures differ before any labor outcome enters. Three versions of the ability-based index alter the weighting used to aggregate occupation--ability links. Three task-based measures use the direct-exposure share (alpha), the direct plus complement-software share (beta), and the broad direct-or-complement share. The source files, transformations, and occupation-level checks are recorded in the replication ledger. Figure~\ref{fig:app-lineage} summarizes the construction paths.

For the task family, the raw primitives satisfy $X_\alpha=D$, $X_\beta=D+S/2$, and $X_\gamma=D+S$. On strict common support, the maximum absolute gap between raw beta and $(\alpha+\gamma)/2$ is $7.0\times10^{-13}$. Mapping and aggregation preserve the identity. Separate employment-weighted standardization changes the relative location and scale and creates a maximum numerical midpoint gap of 0.5112; it does not create a third primitive.

The external-architecture admission rule was written before fitting external outcomes. Webb AI and the reversed OECD capability gap meet the declared construct, public-source, non-title mapping, full-component, distinct-cut, and 80-percent coverage criteria. Webb software remains a computerization control; its source correlation with Webb AI is 0.7021. Frey--Osborne remains an automation-risk comparator, not an AI architecture.

\subsection{What a validation sample would require}

Generated-covariate corrections require information absent here: labels that are closer to the target construct than the indices being evaluated. A useful validation design would sample occupation--task units across all major occupation families and the full distribution of architecture disagreement; oversample split-route occupations and tail-boundary cases; and separately record (i) whether a frontier model can complete the task, (ii) realized time savings with and without complementary software, (iii) actual workplace use, and (iv) task importance and frequency. The distinction prevents capability, complementarity, adoption, and economic weight from being collapsed into one label.

Labels should come from blinded independent domain experts and, where feasible, audited use logs or randomized tool access. Raters should not see the candidate score, occupation outcome, or treatment group. Repeated labels permit inter-rater reliability and adjudication; a held-out set of occupations tests transport across families and taxonomies. The validation release must preserve task text/version, model version and date, tool configuration, rater protocol, occupation crosswalk, and sampling probabilities.

There is no design-free universal sample size. For example, under independent occupation units, a Fisher-$z$ calculation for distinguishing validation correlations .4 and .6 with 80-percent power at a two-sided 5-percent level requires about 112 occupations:
\[
n\simeq 3+\left\{\frac{1.96+.842}{\operatorname{atanh}(.6)-\operatorname{atanh}(.4)}\right\}^{2}.
\]
Task clustering, unequal sampling, rater noise, missing logs, and the desired precision of a corrected downstream coefficient all increase that number. A serious design would report power over assumed prevalence, reliability, intraclass correlation, and holdout share rather than advertise one threshold. The resulting labels could support reliability correction, validation-sample moments, or generated-regressor inference; the current data cannot.
